Lesson 1 — Music Notes & Names

Music Notes

There are 7 basic (natural) notes in the music language, using the first 7 letters of the English alphabet:

A – B – C – D – E – F – G

The note names repeat continuously:

A - B - C - D - E - F - G - A - B - C - D - E - F - G ...

On a keyboard, these are the white keys.

The C Major Scale is one of the first scales most musicians learn. It uses all the basic notes, in this order:

C - D - E - F - G - A - B - C

Sharps & Flats

  • Sharp (♯): raise the note you are playing to the note directly above it.
  • Flat (♭): lower the note you are playing to the note directly below it.

Sharps and flats use the basic/natural note names as their foundation (e.g., C♯ or E♭).

On each black key, there are two note names, because each black key is simultaneously the sharp of one note and the flat of another (e.g., the key between C and D is both C♯ and D♭).

Lesson 2 — Intervals & The Number System

Intervals

An interval = the distance between two notes.

The Number System assigns a number to each note of the major scale (7 notes). In the key of C:

C(1) - D(2) - E(3) - F(4) - G(5) - A(6) - B(7) - C(8)

This is "musical slang" — a note's name can be substituted for its number (e.g., E = the "3", A = the "6").

Three Special Intervals

  • Half (H) Step: move one note to the immediate right or left (e.g., C to C♯, or C to B).
  • Whole (W) Step: two half steps in either direction.
  • Minor (min) 3rd: three half steps in either direction.

The Major Scale Formula

Understanding how the major scale is constructed (via intervals and the number system) — not just memorizing notes — is the foundation for building any chord.

Formula (Whole/Half step pattern):

W - W - H - W - W - W - H

Applied to C Major:

1(C) - W - 2(D) - W - 3(E) - H - 4(F) - W - 5(G) - W - 6(A) - W - 7(B) - H - 1(C)

Lesson 5 — How To Build Chords

Basic Chords are divided into two categories: Triads (3-note chords) and 4-note chords.

  • Triads are formed using 1 – 3 – 5 of the major scale (two exceptions apply — see suspended chords below).
  • 4-note chords add the 6th or 7th note of the major scale to the triad (e.g., 1-3-5-6 or 1-3-5-7).
  • Any note in a triad or 4-note chord can be sharped or flatted except the root (1).

Adding Tensions Worksheet v3.0

Rules for Tensions

  • Tensions are only applied when the 7th of a chord is present.
  • Tension choices: 9th, 11th, 13th.
  • Tensions can be raised (♯) or lowered (♭) a half step — exception: the 11th cannot be flatted (it becomes a 3rd).
  • Multiple tensions can combine, e.g., C maj7(9, ♯11).
  • Tensions are "icing on the cake" — don't overuse.

Phase 4Stacking & Emotional Mapping

Chord Stack System

The Chord Stack System organizes all chords and available tensions into five categories, by increasing complexity, corresponding to gospel-music styles:

  1. Traditional / Hymn — Major triads, Minor triads, Augmented triads, Minor 6th chords, Dominant 7th chords (no tensions)
  2. Worship / CCM — Major triad (add2), Minor triad (add2)
  3. Churchy / Blues & Soul — Minor 7th, Dominant ♭9, 9 & ♯9 chords, diminished chords, diatonic chords
  4. Early Contemporary / RnB — Major7 (9,13), Minor7 (9,11), Dom7sus4 (9,13)
  5. Late Contemporary / Jazz — All chords with all tension possibilities (see Adding Tensions worksheet)

Note: each category includes the chords from all prior categories (e.g., Churchy includes Traditional + Worship/CCM + Churchy).

Chord Stack Combos (CSCs): You can combine categories within a single song — e.g., play Churchy stacks, insert a chord from Modern Contemporary, then return to Churchy. This is how new progressions, approaches, and reharmonizations are created.

Gemini 3.1 Pro · cross-check

This categorization holds up against the frameworks used in professional contemporary keyboard pedagogy, such as Berklee's gospel and R&B curricula. Rather than strict historical boundaries, the list traces the evolution of tension and color in modern playing.

1. Traditional / Hymn

The foundation of four-part SATB harmony. Hymnals lean almost exclusively on primary triads, and a dominant 7th, when it appears, is usually a pure V7 resolving predictably to the tonic.

2. Worship / CCM

The add2 (often felt as an add9) is the hallmark of contemporary Christian music — a wide, anthemic color without the functional pull of a full 7th.

3. Churchy / Blues & Soul

The home of traditional Black gospel and classic soul, built on tension and release: diminished passing chords and altered dominants (♭9, ♯9) produce the gritty "preacher chord" turnarounds.

4. Early Contemporary / RnB

The 90s/early-2000s sound — lush, floating Maj9 and Min11 harmony, with the Dom7sus4 (often voiced as a slash chord) providing a smooth, suspended dominant function.

5. Late Contemporary / Jazz

Modern gospel, neo-soul, and jazz fusion territory, where triads are largely retired in favor of upper-structure extensions, full alterations, and rootless voicings.

The Functional Shift

Moving from level 1 to level 5 requires a shift from thinking in shapes to thinking functionally — by the top level you're managing voice leading and tension resolution, not just naming chords.

Phase 5Reharmonization & Modal Interchange

How To Reharm Songs — The Shared Notes Principle

"The Holy Grail of Crazy Gospel Chords"

Shared Notes Principle: the art of borrowing notes & chords from other keys to use in service of the melody in a song.

Key Rules

  • Any chord borrowed from another key must contain the melody note — either as a basic chord tone (1-3-5-7) or as an available tension (9-11-13), including flatted/sharped versions.
  • Review Chords, tensions, and Common Progressions (Module 1, Lessons 6–8) before attempting this.
  • Always keep the melody note as the top note of every chord to start — this speeds up applying the Shared Notes Principle.

Phase 6Passing Movements

Common Passing Chord Bass Movements

Certain bass movements recur constantly in gospel music — the chord types/stacks used over them can vary, but the bass movement stays constant. Any of the movements below can be used with any Chord Stack category, as long as you honor the MCR formula and always consider the song's melody.

List of Common Passing Chord Bass Movements

  1. ♭5 – 4 – 3
  2. ♭5 – 7 – 3
  3. ♭7 – 6 – ♭6 – 5
  4. 5 – 1 – 4 (or 5 – ♭5 – 4, also the sub-dominant version)
  5. 6 – 2 – 5 – 1 – 4
  6. 3 – ♭3 – 2
  7. 4 – 3 – 2 / 1 – 7 – 6 / ♭7 – 6 – 5 (usually used approaching minor chords — but be creative; if it sounds good, it's fine)

Remember: you can use ANY Chord Stack category over these bass movements, as long as you honor the MCR formula and the melody of the song you're playing.

Chord Stack Passing Movements

Similar principle to the above: bass movements recur regardless of the specific chord stack used over them. All movements below work with any Chord Stack category — honor the MCR formula and always factor in the melody.

Churchy Chord Stacks

  • 1 - 2 - ♯2 - 3
  • 1 - ♭2 - 2 - ♭3 - 3
  • 1 - ♭7 - 6 - ♭6 - 5
  • 4 - ♯4 - 5

Inversions Climb

  • I major → II minor → VII major inversions, over secondary dominants and ii-V-i's / VII-III-VI's, etc.

Contemporary Chord Stacks (Micro Resolutions)

  • Suspended chords → Dominant (♭9) → Minor chords

Worship / CCM Maj add2 Chords

  • 6 - 7 - 1
  • 7 - 1 - 3 - 4/5
  • 6 - 7 - ♭2 - 2

Classical / Traditional Chord Stack Walkdowns

  • ♭6 - ♭5 - 3 - 6
  • 1 - 7 - 6
  • I maj - II min6 - I/3
  • I major - IV min6 - I/3
  • I major - II Major triad/1 - IV min6/1 - 1
